# Seller Financing — where the consumer-mortgage rules actually stop (Phase 13)

**Status:** research complete, NOTHING PUBLISHED. This is a legal boundary and we give no
legal advice, so nothing here reaches a page without a further decision. Recorded now
because the finding corrects a natural and wrong assumption.

**The wrong assumption:** that the consumer mortgage rules turn on whether a property is an
investment or a residence.

## What the rules actually turn on

**Regulation Z's line is UNIT COUNT.** 12 CFR 1026.2(a)(19) defines a dwelling as "a
residential structure that contains one to four units". A 40-unit apartment building is
outside it regardless of who lives there; a fourplex is inside it regardless of investor
intent. The statutory parent, TILA § 103(w) / 15 U.S.C. 1602(w), says the same.

**Business purpose is a SEPARATE and independent filter.** 12 CFR 1026.3(a) excludes credit
extended "primarily for a business, commercial or agricultural purpose". That is a test on
the *credit's* purpose, not the property's character — so a 1–4 unit investment sale exits
by a different door than a 40-unit sale does.

**There is NO seller-financer exclusion from the ability-to-repay rule.** The seller-financer
provisions at 1026.36(a)(4) and (a)(5) — the three-property and one-property tests — sit in
the loan-originator rule only. What actually keeps most seller financers outside ATR is the
creditor definition at 1026.2(a)(17)(v): a person regularly extends consumer credit only
above five dwelling-secured extensions a year.

**Boundary statement.** Federally, the ATR rule and the Reg Z loan-originator rule attach only
where the security is a 1–4 unit dwelling AND the credit is primarily for personal, family or
household purposes. Commercial property is outside because **the rule does not apply** — a
definitional scope failure. A 1–4 unit investment sale is outside because **an exemption
applies**. Those differ in reliability: a scope failure is definitional and robust; an
exemption turns on a purpose determination a creditor can be wrong about.

## The Idaho finding, which is the one that matters

**Idaho does not follow the federal line.** Idaho Code § 26-31-102(16) defines "residential
mortgage loan" as one secured by a dwelling or residential real estate located in Idaho —
**with no personal/family/household limitation at all.** The federal business-purpose escape
hatch therefore has no Idaho counterpart.

- § 26-31-202(2) exempts "An owner of real property who offers credit secured by a contract of
  sale, mortgage, or deed of trust on the property sold" — unconditional, no property count.
  But it is expressly limited to "this part" (Part 2, brokers and lenders).
- § 26-31-304 (Part 3, originator licensing) has four exemptions: registered MLOs, immediate
  family, the individual's own residence, and attorneys. **No seller-financing exemption.**

## Citation hazards recorded

- **Idaho Title 26 Chapter 31 sections are numbered `26-31-1xx`, NOT `26-31xx`.** `26-3102`
  returns a 404. Given the § 63-307A fabrication, this is exactly the shape of error to avoid.
- § 50-1402 is titled "Declaration of value of property" and is a **false friend** — Title 50
  chapter 14 governs cities conveying their own real property. Nothing to do with private sales.
- **SB 1196 (2007)** keeps surfacing in search carrying quotable transfer-tax and disclosure
  language. It died in committee. It is not law. It is the same bill behind § 63-307A.
