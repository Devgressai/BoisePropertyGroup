## The tax section (highest risk, read this first)

What the IRS pages say, as of 2026-11-13:

- Topic No. 431 ("Page Last Reviewed or Updated: 24-Sep-2026") lists the qualified principal residence
  indebtedness (QPRI) exclusion as covering debt "discharged before January 1, 2026, or discharged subject
  to an arrangement that is entered into and evidenced in writing before January 1, 2026".
- Publication 4681 is the **2025 edition** ("For use in preparing 2025 Returns"). Its What's New says QPRI
  "cannot be excluded from income for discharges completed or discharge agreements entered into after
  December 31, 2025."
- Instructions for Forms 1099-A and 1099-C ("Revised: 04/2025") say the exclusion "expires on December 31, 2025".

So the three pages agree, and for a cancellation in 2026 the IRS's published position is that the QPRI
exclusion is not available unless a written arrangement predates 2026. The page says exactly that,
attributed and dated, and no more.

What is NOT settled, and is said in the page:

- No 2026-edition Pub 4681 existed at access. The 2026 statement rests on a forward-looking note in the 2025
  edition plus Topic 431's September 2026 review.
- Congress can change this after the fact. I did not source the history of the exclusion (earlier expiries
  and extensions), so the page does not describe it. Pub 4681's own "Future Developments" line points to
  IRS.gov/Pub4681 for later legislation; I did not fetch that landing page.
- Re-read all three IRS pages before every republication, and again in January/February 2027 when 2026
  forms and the new Pub 4681 publish. If Congress extends the exclusion, the section is wrong the same day.

Other tax traps:

- **Idaho state income tax** on canceled debt was not sourced. The page says it covers federal tax only.
- Recourse vs nonrecourse: the IRS distinction is described, but nothing I read says how an Idaho home loan
  is classified for federal tax. The page does not say. Do not let an editor add it.
- The deemed-sale rule can also create a gain or loss on the house itself (Pub 4681 ch. 2, Pub 523 home-sale
  exclusion). I did not claim any of that; the page only says the tax treatment "depends".
- $600 is the creditor's Form 1099-C filing threshold. It is not a tax-free amount. The prose says so.
- The insolvency test in Pub 4681 counts exempt assets such as retirement accounts. The page gives the
  one-sentence definition only and applies it to nobody.
- IRS spells it "canceled"; the page follows the IRS.

## Deficiency section

- **45-1512 and 6-108 are about judgments after a FORECLOSURE SALE.** Neither mentions a short sale or a deed
  in lieu. I could not find an Idaho statute that addresses a deficiency after a short sale or deed in lieu,
  and I did not read case law. The page says the outcome comes from the written agreement and relies on the
  CFPB's "get the waiver in writing" advice. The CFPB says "in some states" a lender could sue after a short
  sale; it does not say whether Idaho is one. The page says that plainly.
- I did NOT claim Idaho Code 45-1503(1), which restricts when a trust-deed beneficiary may bring a judicial
  action on the obligation (after a trustee's sale under 45-1512, judicial foreclosure, "substantially
  valueless" security, or a 6-101(3) excluded action). I read it; it is cached. It bears on whether a lender
  can sue on the note without foreclosing, but explaining it gets close to applying law to the reader, and it
  sits in the foreclosure-timeline guide's scope. A reviewer may want one attributed sentence.
- 6-101's "one action" rule has fifteen carve-outs in subsection (3). I quoted the rule and the deficiency
  clause only, and did not characterise it as a protection.
- I did not source which instrument most Idaho home loans use (deed of trust vs mortgage). The page says
  "depends on the document that secures your loan".
- 6-101 also says judicial foreclosure sales are subject to redemption. Left out (other guides' scope).
- "3 months" in 45-1512 is a numeral in the statute, quoted as such.

## Definitions and HUD

- Definitions are CFPB consumer-education text, not statute. Idaho Code sections read here do not define
  "short sale". The CFPB pages carry a not-legal-advice disclaimer.
- HUD: the only clear current page found was hud.gov/helping-americans/fha-loss-mitigation. Several older
  HUD URLs returned 404 (site restructure). The page has no revision date, lists no eligibility conditions
  and gives no length for the PFS marketing period. HUD Handbook 4000.1 and the Mortgagee Letters were not
  read. Both HUD claims are tagged FHA-only and the prose keeps that qualifier.
- Nothing was sourced for VA, USDA, Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac short sale / deed in lieu programs. Left out.
- The CFPB counselor quote contains the CFPB's typo "though a HUD-approved agency". Verbatim in the claim,
  corrected to "through" in prose (paraphrase, not a quotation).

## Left out because unsourced

- Credit report effects of a short sale vs deed in lieu vs foreclosure, and any waiting period before a new
  mortgage. The CFPB deed-in-lieu page only says both a leftover balance and foreclosure "can affect your
  ability to purchase another home"; I did not build a claim on that.
- How long a lender takes to approve a short sale. No day count anywhere.
- What happens to second mortgages, HOA liens or judgment liens in a deed in lieu (lenders commonly refuse
  when junior liens exist; I have no primary source, so it is not on the page).
- Whether a pending short sale / deed in lieu request delays a scheduled trustee's sale. Federal servicing
  rules (12 CFR 1024.41) deal with foreclosure while a loss mitigation application is pending; that is the
  avoid-foreclosure guide's scope and I did not read it. The page says it does not cover the question. After
  merge the editor should link that sentence to the avoid-foreclosure guide.
- Real estate commissions and who pays closing costs in a short sale.
